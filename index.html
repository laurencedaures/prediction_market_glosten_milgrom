Market Making on a Binary Contract

How a market maker on Kalshi or Polymarket sets her two prices: a fair value, a spread against informed traders, a skew for the inventory she is carrying, and a grid she cannot quote inside. Each term is derived, then plotted. Move the sliders.

Why a binary contract is the easy case

In an equity market the fundamental value is unobservable and unbounded, so a dealer models it. Here the contract pays $1 or $0, so its value is a Bernoulli draw: the expected value is the probability, and the variance is fixed by it.

v ∈ {0, 1}  ·  E[v] = p  ·  Var[v] = p(1 − p)

Everything below follows from that one line. Both the spread the maker needs and the skew she applies turn out to be proportional to p(1 − p), which vanishes at the wings — and that is where the exchange's price grid stops her.

1

The spread against informed traders

Glosten and Milgrom (1985). A fraction α of orders come from traders who already know the outcome; the rest are uninformed and buy or sell with equal chance. The maker cannot tell them apart, so she quotes prices that are already conditioned on the direction of the order she is about to receive.

P(buy | v=1) = 1 + α2    P(buy | v=0) = 1 − α2

Bayes' rule turns those into the two quotes. The ask is what the contract is worth given that someone is buying it from her:

a = E[v | buy] = p(1 + α)p(1 + α) + (1 − p)(1 − α)

b = E[v | sell] = p(1 − α)p(1 − α) + (1 − p)(1 + α)

Subtracting them collapses to something very simple:

s = a − b = 4α p(1 − p)1 − α²(2p − 1)²  ≈  4α p(1 − p)

The approximation is accurate to better than 1% for α below about 0.1. Two consequences. The spread is widest at 50¢ and collapses at the wings, in absolute terms. And it is zero when α = 0: with no informed traders a competitive maker quotes a single price. The spread is the price of ignorance, nothing else — no inventory, no costs, no profit.

2

The skew for inventory

A maker who has been bought from repeatedly ends up short, and a prediction market gives her no easy way out: she usually cannot hedge, and the position settles at $0 or $1. So she stops quoting symmetrically around p and starts quoting around the price at which she is indifferent to holding one more contract.

With mean–variance preferences over terminal wealth and a position of q contracts:

U(q) = c + qp − γ2 q² p(1 − p)

Differentiating gives the reservation price, the value of one marginal contract to her:

r = p − γ q p(1 − p)

She then quotes r ± s/2 instead of p ± s/2. Long inventory pushes both quotes down, so she is more likely to be sold to than bought from, and the position drains back towards zero. She is not forecasting; she is pricing her own risk.

Note that the skew carries the same p(1 − p) factor as the spread. At the wings a maker is almost indifferent to inventory in absolute terms, because a 3¢ contract cannot move far against her. Both of her adjustments shrink together.

3

The grid, and where the money comes from

The maker now has a target: quote r ± s/2. The exchange only lets her name prices on a grid, and the two quotes must differ by at least one tick. Her realised spread is therefore

srealised = max( s, one tick )

This is the whole argument in one line. Where s > tick the grid is irrelevant and she earns, in expectation, nothing: that is the Glosten–Milgrom zero-profit result, and it is a genuine prediction, not an accident of the model. What she collects from uninformed traders she hands back to informed ones.

Where the tick binds she is forced to quote wider than the information justifies, and the gap is a rent:

E[profit per contract] = max(0, tick − s)2

She cannot be undercut, because a competitor would have to improve by a whole tick and would then be quoting inside fair value. Competition moves to the only free dimension left, the queue: makers race to be early at the same price rather than to post a better one. The rent is real but it is dissipated in speed.

Two caveats worth stating in class. The model assumes competitive makers, so any observed profit beyond this is market power, a fee rebate, or order flow that is biased rather than merely uninformed — which is exactly what Bartlett and O'Hara argue happens on Kalshi, where takers lean towards YES. And it assumes the maker knows α; in practice estimating the informed share is the hard part of the job.

Does Kalshi's taper remove the rent?

Share of the price range on which the grid binds, and the largest rent anywhere on it. A finer tick can only shrink max(0, tick − s), so the taper removes the maker's rent rather than moving it — but only at the wings, which is where it applies.

4

The quoting rule, assembled

Putting the three terms together gives something a student can implement in a few lines, and it is close to what the bots in the companion simulators run.

1. Estimate the fair value  p  from the order flow and the news.
2. Spread for information:  s = 4α p(1 − p).
3. Reservation price for inventory:  r = p − γ q p(1 − p).
4. Target quotes:  bid = r − s/2,   ask = r + s/2.
5. Round to the grid:  bid down, ask up, and force ask − bid ≥ one tick.
6. Withdraw entirely when α jumps — before a scheduled announcement, or when the flow turns one-sided.

What the model leaves out

Step 6 is the one the algebra cannot deliver: a static model has no way to represent "something is about to happen". Also absent are the maker's capital constraint, which on a fully collateralized exchange locks the full $1 behind every pair of contracts; competition between makers for the queue, which decides who actually gets the rent in step 3; fees and rebates, which on Kalshi fall on takers and so change the flow a maker sees rather than her own economics; and the possibility that the uninformed flow is biased rather than balanced, which breaks the zero-profit result in the maker's favour.

Application questions

1. The quotes, by hand
Take p = 0.30 and α = 0.05. Compute the ask, the bid and the spread from the exact formulas, then check against 4αp(1 − p).
Ask = 0.30(1.05) / [0.30(1.05) + 0.70(0.95)] = 0.315 / 0.980 = 0.3214. Bid = 0.30(0.95) / [0.30(0.95) + 0.70(1.05)] = 0.285 / 1.020 = 0.2794. Spread = 4.20¢. The approximation gives 4(0.05)(0.30)(0.70) = 4.20¢: identical to three figures.
2. Where does the grid bind?
With α = 0.05 and a flat 1¢ tick, solve for the prices at which the maker's desired spread equals one tick. What fraction of the 1–99¢ range is affected?
Set 4(0.05)p(1 − p) = 0.01, so p(1 − p) = 0.05, giving p = 5.3% or 94.7%. The grid binds on roughly 10% of the price range. Small — but on Kalshi about two thirds of contracts trade in exactly that region, so it binds on most of the volume. That mismatch between range and volume is the argument for the taper.
3. The same question with the taper
Repeat with a 0.1¢ tick at the wings. Where does the grid bind now?
4(0.05)p(1 − p) = 0.001 gives p(1 − p) = 0.005, so p ≈ 0.5% or 99.5%. The binding region nearly disappears, and the maker can quote the spread the information justifies almost everywhere. Note the taper does not help her earn more — it removes a rent. It helps the taker.
4. Reading the zero-profit result
The model says a competitive maker earns zero where the tick does not bind. Yet real market makers are profitable. Give three reasons the two are not in contradiction.
First, the uninformed flow may not be balanced: if takers systematically buy YES, the maker sells it above fair value and keeps the difference, which is the optimism tax in Bartlett and O'Hara. Second, makers are not perfectly competitive — queue position, speed and exchange liquidity programmes all create rents. Third, the maker may simply estimate p better than the flow she trades against, in which case she is not a pure maker at all but an informed trader who happens to quote.
5. Inventory at the wings
With γ = 0.004, how far does a long position of 200 contracts move the reservation price at p = 0.50, and at p = 0.05? Comment.
At 0.50: γqp(1 − p) = 0.004 × 200 × 0.25 = 0.20, a shift of 20¢, enormous. At 0.05: 0.004 × 200 × 0.0475 = 0.038, a shift of 3.8¢ — but on a 5¢ contract that is most of its value, and it would push her quotes below zero. The lesson is that γ must be read against the contract's own price: the same risk aversion means something very different at the two ends of the range.
6. Why not just quote wider?
A maker worried about informed traders could simply double her spread. What does the model say she gives up, and what does she gain?
She gains protection: the informed trader's profit per trade is capped by the spread she quotes, so a wider spread transfers less to him. She gives up the uninformed flow, which is the only flow she makes money from, and she gives up queue position to any competitor willing to quote tighter. The optimal spread is the one that just compensates her for the adverse selection she expects, which is precisely s = 4αp(1 − p). Quoting wider is not safer in equilibrium; it is simply not trading.

The model is standard: Glosten and Milgrom (1985) for the information term, and an inventory term in the spirit of Ho and Stoll (1981) and Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008), specialised to a Bernoulli payoff.
